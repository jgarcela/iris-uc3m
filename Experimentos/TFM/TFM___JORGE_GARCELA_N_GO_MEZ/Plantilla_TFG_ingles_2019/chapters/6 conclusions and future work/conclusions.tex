%----------
%	CONCLUSIONS
%----------	

In this work, we have addressed the challenge of detecting hate speech against women in social media, particularly in the context of the \textit{"\#SeAcabó"} movement, through different approaches: Machine Learning, Deep Learning and Generative AI. In particular, this study focused on three specific classification tasks: "Análisis General", which aimed to detect negative comments directed at women, especially to the Spanish National Football Team players; "Contenido Negativo", which focused on identifying the types of negative content present in those negative comments, and "Insultos", which involved classifying insults within negative comments into different categories.

The complexities of language, particularly in Spanish, and the way it is used in society presented significant challenges in detecting hate speech, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:data_preprocessing}. In particular, one major challenge was that insults do not always have a negative connotation and can sometimes be used positively (e.g., \textit{"eres la puta ama"}--eng. `You are the fucking boss'--). Additionally, other challenges were present in the dataset due to the nature of tweets, which are often informal and contain slang words, spelling errors, and hashtags, which make it difficult to estimate the intention and meaning. Moreover, the 280-character limit of tweets often leads to ambiguity, as users condense complex thoughts into a limited space. Another significant challenge, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:class_imbalance}, is the high imbalance as well as topical and lexical biases present in HS detection datasets \cite{liebowitz-2024}, which added an extra layer of complexity to the classification.

To address these challenges, we began by applying state-of-the-art preprocessing techniques to our dataset as well as tackling class imbalance. Secondly, we employed various text encoding techniques, ranging from traditional methods like \textit{Bag of Words (BoW)} and \textit{TFIDF} to more sophisticated approaches such as transformer-based models (\textit{RoBERTa, BETO}) and OpenAI's text embeddings (\textit{text-embedding-3-large}). With the data prepared, we implemented models across the three mentioned approaches (ML, DL and GenAI), allowing us to compare their effectiveness, and provide insights into the current state as well as their strengths and limitations for this work. In particular, for the DL approach, we incorporated "contextual information" as detailed in Section~\ref{implementation_dl}, which involved adding context to the classification, such as the original tweet of \textit{"\#SeAcabó"} (Figure~\ref{tweet_seacabo}), and the information of the situation itself. On the other hand, for the GenAI approach, we decided to test the zero-shot learning capabilities of different LLMs in detecting HS.

Despite these challenges, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, we achieved notable results for the three types of classification tasks, particularly for "Análisis General", obtaining a high accuracy of 0.946, with F1-Scores of 0.966 and 0.878 for "Comentario Positivo" and "Comentario Negativo". However, we observed a significant decrease in accuracy to 0.634 in the "Contenido Negativo" task, reflected by a weighted average F1-Score of 0.630, and an even lower F1-Score for the "Insultos" class (0.455). On the other hand, accuracy improved to 0.738 in the "Insultos" task, with a weighted average F1-Score of 0.740, and over 0.609 in all classes. Although we did not achieve high accuracy for the "Contenido Negativo" and "Insultos" tasks, we remain confident in our models and believe that further research is needed in these classifications, as the classes within them are closely similar in meaning, making them particularly challenging to distinguish.

Additionally, results from Section~\ref{sec:discussion} suggest that the ML approach, despite its simplicity, consistently provided the best overall performance, particularly in the more complex tasks of "Contenido Negativo" and "Insultos". This success is partly due to the extensive number of experiments and the wide range of hyperparameters used, allowing the models to be tested for tons of combinations. On the other hand, the DL approach demonstrated strong potential, particularly in leveraging contextual information. However, this came with trade-offs in computational cost and the limited number of hyperparameters we were able to test. Additionally, for the GenAI approach we believe that while zero-shot performed better than expected given its lack of training, it still requires refinement to match the consistency and reliability of the other approaches.

Finally, as commented in Section~\ref{sec:justification_solution}, the growing impact and popularity of this approach have led to an increase in research and proposed solutions in almost any imaginable field. While it excels at performing a wide range of different tasks, it still raises important concerns about data privacy, ethical issues and bias. Therefore, despite that these automatic systems can significantly help in processing large amounts of data, we consider that humans remain indispensable and must continue to review and validate the outputs and decisions these models produce. That is the reason why in Section~\ref{sec:socioeconomic_impact} we propose our work as a preliminary filter in the HS detection procedure before the actual human classification. This approach is particularly important in the context of our work, as noted in Section~\ref{sec:hs_society}, since hate speech content is difficult to define accurately on a global basis, is often subjective, and in our specific case, involves classification categories that frequently overlap.
